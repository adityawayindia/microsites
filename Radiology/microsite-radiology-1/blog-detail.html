What Your MRI Report Actually Says

Dr. Vikram Rao

Consultant Radiologist

Updated on 20 Apr 2026

An imaging report is written first for your treating doctor, which is why it can read like a foreign language to the person it is actually about. The structure, though, is always the same, and once you know what each section is for, most of the anxiety around it falls away.

1) Clinical Indication

The opening line records why the scan was requested. It matters more than patients expect: the indication decides which sequences are run and what the radiologist looks hardest at. A vague referral produces a vague report, so it is always worth telling us your full symptom history.

2) Technique

This paragraph lists the sequences performed, the field strength, and whether contrast was given. If you ever need the same region imaged again, this section lets the next radiologist reproduce the study exactly and compare like with like.

3) Findings

The longest section, and the one most likely to alarm you. It describes everything visible, normal and abnormal, in anatomical order. Incidental notes such as a small cyst or mild disc bulge are extremely common and are recorded for completeness, not because they need treatment.

4) Impression

This is the section to read first. It answers the clinical question in a few lines, ranks the likely diagnoses, and recommends any further imaging or follow-up interval. If the impression and your symptoms do not line up, that is exactly the thing to raise with your doctor.

Words That Sound Worse Than They Are

"Unremarkable" means normal. "Nonspecific" means the finding is real but does not point to one diagnosis on its own. "Clinical correlation is advised" is not a hedge — it is a request for your doctor to read the images alongside your examination and blood work before deciding anything.

When To Ask For A Review

Ask for a sit-down review of your images if the report mentions a finding you do not understand, if it recommends a follow-up scan without a clear interval, or if a previous study was not available for comparison. Bringing prior scans, even old ones on disc, often changes the reading.
